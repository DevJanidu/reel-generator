# ODD FRAME — 2D Still Graphic Design Guide

## 1. Purpose and scope

Create original, instantly readable 2D story illustrations for **ODD FRAME — Small Stories. Big Emotions.** Each image must communicate a specific story beat and direct attention toward the information that matters now.

This is an art-direction guide for still images, not an animation guide. Apply it to image-generation prompts and manual design review. It is a portable Markdown reference; it does not install itself as an app skill.

The brand is entertainment-first. Stories may be funny, mysterious, romantic, frightening, sad, adventurous, clever, or fantastical. Never force a moral, therapy message, or motivational ending. Never make every genre look like horror.

Core visual formula:

**Quiet environment + readable character + one yellow point of attention + a meaningful story situation.**

Use selective color, controlled brightness, and composition together. Yellow is a design choice, not a guarantee of attention, memory, or virality.

## 2. Mandatory illustration style

- Strictly 2D, with illustrated characters, scenery, objects, lighting, and shadows.
- Confident dark ink contours; simple, expressive faces; clear silhouettes.
- Flat local colors and limited stepped shading. Soft illustrated light is allowed, but forms must remain visibly drawn.
- Restrained paper grain, hatching, or brush texture. Keep faces and clues cleaner than peripheral scenery.
- Layered foreground, midground, and background for depth without photographic rendering.
- Expressive stylized animals, simple stickmen, and occasional fictional creatures. Use only the approved cast for the story.
- Preserve approved anatomy and character identity. Stickmen have two arms and two legs; animals retain their species-appropriate anatomy unless explicitly designed otherwise.
- No photorealism, 3D, CGI, photographic materials, realistic human facial anatomy, or mixed rendering styles.
- Do not reproduce another page's characters, exact scenes, recurring compositions, or decorative motifs. Build original story-specific settings.

“Cinematic” means intentional framing, mood, staging, and light—not realistic rendering.

## 3. Fixed palette and exact responsibilities

| Role | Hex | Where to use it | Where to avoid it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Midnight charcoal | #14171C | Deep background, foreground framing, shadow masses | Large areas hiding essential gestures or faces |
| Muted slate | #41464E | Walls, ground, furniture, distant trees, secondary clothes | High-contrast patterns behind the clue |
| Lifted gray | #7D838B | Readable secondary objects and shaded character details | Broad highlights competing with the focal point |
| Warm ivory | #F1EBDD | Faces, eye whites, small readable features, selected light surfaces | Large pure-white scenery that steals attention |
| Golden yellow | #FFD43B | One important object, interaction zone, opening, or clue | Random decorative objects across the frame |
| Pale yellow | #FFF2B0 | Tiny highlight, light core, small reflection | Large washed-out areas or every face |
| Muted ochre | #A98428 | Yellow material in shadow or subdued reflected warmth | Replacing all environmental gray with brown |

Hex codes describe targets; generated images may approximate them. Judge the resulting hierarchy visually. Apply exact palette correction in an editor when required.

Starting color budget: approximately 70–85% subdued environment, 10–25% readable characters/supporting forms, and 3–10% strong yellow accents. These are flexible visual targets, not pixel quotas. A doorway or pool of light can require more area; keep only one dominant attention zone.

Preserve identifying animal fur and costume colors in muted form. Do not turn every character gray or yellow if that destroys identity. Yellow should remain the strongest saturated accent.

## 4. Decide what gets yellow before writing the prompt

Complete this sentence first:

**The viewer must notice ______ first because ______ changes or explains the story.**

Choose one primary target. Use the following order of preference:

1. A meaningful clue or object: key, envelope, ticket, missing item, unusual footprint.
2. A critical action or relationship: the handoff, the empty seat, the distance between characters.
3. An important destination or boundary: doorway, window, path, threshold.
4. The protagonist, only when their expression or state is the actual story information.

Do not automatically highlight the biggest character, the narrator, the villain, or the center of the frame. Highlight what the audience needs to understand at this beat.

### Choose one color mechanism

| Mechanism | Use when | Placement instructions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yellow material | The object can naturally be yellow | Make the object's surface golden yellow; use ochre shadows; do not add emitted light |
| Selective saturation | A colored clue must stand out in a quiet scene | Keep the target saturated and other colors subdued; maintain normal shading |
| Motivated illumination | A lamp, window, lantern, or magical source exists | Put yellow in the source, a restrained surrounding pool, and nearby light-facing surfaces |
| Background separation | The subject must retain its own colors | Place a yellow-lit wall, doorway, or small atmosphere patch behind its silhouette |
| Interaction spotlight | The exchange or empty space matters most | Place the yellow pool under or behind the interaction; keep the participants readable |

Prefer one mechanism per scene. Combine two only when they support the same focal area, such as a lantern and the face it lights.

## 5. Exact color placement rules

### Main object

If the clue is a yellow object, put the strongest saturation on its visible surface. Use a pale highlight only on a small light-facing edge. Keep the silhouette distinct from its surroundings. A yellow paper letter is not a lamp: it must not cast yellow light on the room.

### Characters

Keep the face, gesture, and identifying clothes readable. Use ivory or a light species-appropriate face tone, but reduce unnecessary highlights if the face competes with a smaller clue. Yellow belongs on a character only when it is an approved costume element, a story clue, or reflected light from an actual source.

Do not add a bright yellow scarf to every protagonist as an automatic branding device. That would pull attention away from whichever object matters next.

### Ground

A localized yellow pool may anchor the event if an actual light source motivates it. The brightest ground should be near that source; fade toward slate and charcoal. Avoid a giant glowing circle beneath every subject. A matte yellow object alone does not justify a light pool.

### Background

Use low-saturation slate and charcoal with enough separation to read the setting. A yellow doorway or lit window may frame the subject when it is narratively relevant. Suppress unrelated bright windows, streetlights, flowers, and signs.

### Foreground

Use dark, simple shapes to frame the event without enclosing every image in the same black tunnel. Keep foreground texture low. Do not cover hands, faces, or the clue.

### Reflected light

Reflected yellow appears only on surfaces facing a real source and decreases with distance. Use a restrained warm edge on nearby fur, clothing, furniture, or ground. Do not outline every contour in yellow. Keep shadows consistent with the source direction.

### Typography

No generated captions, labels, speech bubbles, or decorative text unless the storyboard explicitly requests them. If text is required, reserve clean space and add it in the editor for accuracy. Keep it short, high contrast, and clear of the main clue. Avoid a large yellow headline competing with a yellow object.

## 6. Value hierarchy: brightness as well as color

Plan three readable value groups:

- Dark: environment and framing.
- Middle: supporting objects, clothing, and secondary scenery.
- Light: essential face details and the story focal area.

The most important area should have the strongest useful combination of saturation, local contrast, placement, and size. It need not contain the single brightest pixel. A tiny face highlight is acceptable if it does not distract.

Avoid pure black swallowing the action, pure white scenery stealing attention, and heavy bloom erasing object edges. Preserve clear silhouettes.

Perform a grayscale check: the event must still be understandable without color. Yellow reinforces the design; it cannot repair an unreadable silhouette or hidden clue.

## 7. Composition and mobile framing

Default master canvas: 1080 × 1920, vertical 9:16, full bleed. Higher-resolution artwork is acceptable at the same ratio.

Planning guides for this canvas:

- Keep essential story information mainly within x=90–890 and y=200–1490.
- Keep critical clues away from the rightmost 160 px and bottom 350 px where controls and captions may appear.
- Treat these as conservative working margins, not fixed Facebook specifications. Verify the final edit in the actual interface and adapt to its current overlays.
- Let nonessential scenery extend to the canvas edges. Do not leave visible safe-area boxes.
- Use a medium or close framing when a small clue or expression drives the beat. Do not solve every scene with a distant full-body shot.
- Place the clue and character so their relationship reads immediately. Avoid accidental tangencies and overlapping silhouettes.
- Use gaze, gestures, paths, furniture edges, and light direction to lead toward the focal area.
- Use negative space around the clue. Simplify detail behind it.
- Vary framing across the story: establishing view, interaction, detail, reaction, consequence. Keep the same drawing language.

Thumbnail check: inspect the image at roughly 135 × 240 px. Can you recognize the event, character relationship, and important object? If not, enlarge or simplify the relevant information. Do not merely increase glow.

## 8. Environment and texture

Create enough setting detail to establish location, mood, and cause-and-effect. Every prominent prop should explain the place, support the action, or plant a clue.

Use simpler detail in distant scenery and more purposeful detail near the event. Keep background edges softer or lower contrast than important contours. Texture must survive mobile reduction without turning into visual noise.

Do not default to cracked earth, dead trees, chains, prisons, smoke, or haunted buildings. Those belong only in stories that need them. Kitchens, stations, forests, offices, beaches, and bright outdoor scenes can all use the same selective-yellow system.

## 9. Genre controls

| Genre | Environment and light | Yellow's role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mystery | Cool slate shadows, hidden space, selective detail | Unusual clue, opening, or revealed evidence |
| Comedy | Lifted grays, open staging, readable expressions | Absurd prop, mismatch, or punchline object |
| Romance/friendship | Softer charcoal, gentle warm light | Shared object, invitation, or point of connection |
| Sadness | Quiet negative space and restrained contrast | Absence-related object or surviving connection |
| Adventure | Strong silhouettes, directional scenery | Destination, tool, or route marker |
| Horror | Deep but readable shadows, limited visibility | Warning, source of uncertainty, or dangerous threshold |
| Betrayal/revenge | Clear opposing positions and controlled contrast | Evidence, exchanged item, or consequence |

Yellow must not always mean happiness, safety, or goodness. Its consistent brand function is “look here.” The story supplies its meaning.

## 10. Continuity across stills

Before the first image, lock the cast descriptions, proportions, outline style, clothing, identifying colors, important props, location layout, and palette.

Across consecutive stills:

- Preserve the object's actual color. If the yellow envelope stops being the focal point, lower its brightness or prominence naturally; do not recolor it gray.
- Preserve light direction and source position unless the scene changes.
- Preserve prop count, damage, open/closed states, and character positions logically.
- Keep left/right geography understandable. Use a new establishing view after a major change when needed.
- Keep a face reference and full-body reference for recurring characters within the story.
- Repeat full approved descriptions in standalone generation prompts when the tool cannot reliably retain context.
- Transfer attention deliberately: yellow object → readable reaction → newly revealed yellow clue. Do not add unrelated yellow decorations to fill the frame.

Each new still must add information: action, reaction, clue, choice, obstacle, consequence, or reveal. Avoid consecutive images that repeat the same story state with only a different camera angle.

## 12. Master image prompt template

Replace every bracketed field. Do not send unresolved placeholders.

```text
Create one original vertical 9:16 cinematic 2D story still for
ODD FRAME — Small Stories. Big Emotions.

STORY MOMENT:
[One concrete visible event. Describe observable action rather than
only abstract feelings. Include the clue, question, or payoff.]

CHARACTERS AND CONTINUITY:
[Full approved descriptions, expressions, poses, references, prop
counts and states. State what must match the preceding still.]

ILLUSTRATION:
Strictly hand-drawn 2D characters and environments. Confident dark
ink contours, simplified expressive faces, clear silhouettes, flat
local colors, limited illustrated shading, restrained paper texture,
and layered scenery. Keep faces and story clues visually clean.
All elements share the same 2D drawing treatment.

COMPOSITION:
[Shot size and angle.] Place [character] at [position] and [clue]
at [position]. The viewer should notice [target] first, then
[secondary information]. Use [gaze/gesture/environmental line] to
connect them. Simplify the area behind the clue. Keep essential
information clear of the right-side controls and bottom caption zone.

PALETTE AND EXACT COLOR PLACEMENT:
Deep charcoal #14171C and muted slate #41464E dominate [areas].
Golden yellow #FFD43B is concentrated on [specific surface/area].
Use [chosen yellow mechanism]. Keep [secondary objects] subdued.
Use warm ivory #F1EBDD for [readable facial/features areas].
Preserve [identifying character colors] in muted form.
Pale yellow #FFF2B0 is limited to [tiny highlight, or omit].
No competing saturated accents and no overall yellow filter.

LIGHT:
[Source, location, direction, softness, and intended shadows.]
[If emissive: specify the nearby light-facing surfaces receiving
restrained yellow spill. If matte: explicitly say the yellow object
does not emit light or create a glowing halo.]

MOOD:
[Genre and specific emotion.] Match the lighting and staging to it.

EXCLUDE:
Photorealism, 3D, CGI, photographic textures, realistic human faces,
heavy bloom, random glowing objects, excessive detail, inconsistent
anatomy, altered character designs, text, logos, and watermarks.
```

## 13. Worked color-placement examples

### A. Mystery — a key inside a locked box

An ivory-faced rabbit stands left of a slate table. On the table is a transparent locked box containing a golden-yellow key. The rabbit holds an identical key in muted ochre. Use a medium shot so the two keys and the lock read clearly. Yellow material belongs on the enclosed key; the held key remains recognizably the same color under dimmer light. The box receives neutral illustrated highlights. No yellow emission, light pool, or floating particles. Keep surrounding shelves dark and simple.

First look: enclosed key. Second look: matching key and confused expression. Question: why are there two?

### B. Sadness — the empty chair

Two chairs sit at a small slate kitchen table; one is empty. A golden-yellow scarf hangs over the empty chair. A muted brown bear sits at the other side, looking toward it. Soft neutral window light makes the scarf readable without making it glow. Leave quiet negative space around the empty chair. Do not illuminate the bear's entire body yellow or add other yellow kitchen objects.

First look: scarf and empty chair. Second look: bear's reaction. The visual relationship carries the feeling.

### C. Comedy — the tiny boss

A tiny ivory mouse sits high in an oversized charcoal office chair. A golden-yellow nameplate faces the viewer, with no generated lettering. A much larger wolf waits with a folder in the slate doorway. Lift the room's gray values so the size mismatch is immediately visible. Use yellow as a compact accent at the mouse's desk; no sinister glow or horror framing.

First look: tiny mouse at the executive desk. Second look: nervous wolf. The scale contrast supplies the joke.

### D. Adventure — the distant doorway

A small fox stands on a slate ridge facing a distant open doorway in dark ruins. The doorway emits golden-yellow light with a pale small core; nearby stone edges and ground receive restrained illumination. Keep the rest of the sky and ruins subdued. Preserve enough doorway size to read on a phone. Do not place a second bright moon, campfire, or yellow costume on the fox.

First look: destination. Second look: fox and route. Here illumination, rather than yellow material, is the mechanism.

## 14. Review and repair

| Problem | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Entire image looks yellow | Return large surfaces to slate/charcoal; isolate the accent |
| Everything glows | Keep emission only on actual sources; remove arbitrary halos |
| Face overwhelms the clue | Reduce face highlight, enlarge clue, improve its local contrast |
| Clue disappears on mobile | Use closer framing, cleaner silhouette, and simpler surroundings |
| Image feels muddy | Separate dark, middle, and light value groups; reduce texture |
| Every story looks frightening | Lift environment values, open the staging, remove horror motifs |
| Character looks different | Restore reference proportions and exact approved features |
| Yellow prop changes color between stills | Restore material color; alter lighting or framing instead |
| Two regions compete | Choose the primary target and subdue the other region |
| Style becomes realistic | Reinforce flat local color, ink contours, simplified anatomy, and illustrated shading |

Final approval checklist:

- [ ] One clear story beat and one primary attention zone.
- [ ] Yellow has a specific narrative purpose and exact placement.
- [ ] Material color and emitted light are treated differently.
- [ ] Faces, gestures, and clue remain readable at thumbnail size.
- [ ] The event still reads in grayscale.
- [ ] Strictly 2D rendering throughout.
- [ ] Approved cast, anatomy, props, and lighting continuity preserved.
- [ ] Genre is supported without forcing a moral or horror mood.
- [ ] Essential information avoids expected interface overlays.
- [ ] No unwanted text, logos, watermarks, or decorative clutter.

If a still fails, revise the composition or color placement before adding effects. Do not use more glow as the default repair.

## 15. How to use this guide

Provide this file alongside the story script, approved character references, and scene brief. Ask the image-prompt writer to apply it to every still and explicitly state each scene's focal target, yellow mechanism, and yellow placement. Review actual generated images against the checklist; prompt compliance alone is not approval.
